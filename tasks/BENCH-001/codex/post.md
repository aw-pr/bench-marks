# The Thousand Flowers Have Wilted

Financial services AI is no longer a use-case hunt. It is an operating model test, and most firms are built to fail it.

HSBC decommissioned 1,165 legacy applications in 2025. That number tells us more about financial services AI than another assistant demo ever will.

The public story has been about models, prompts and agents. The operational story is duller, which usually means it is closer to the money. Retire old systems. Concentrate governance. Choose business domains. Train managers. Put model risk at the gate rather than in the post-incident review. Stop asking scattered teams to prove magic in a corner of the estate while the real constraints sit untouched.

This is the shift now visible across financial services. The pilot era is ending, but not because the pilots all worked. It is ending because too many of them did not matter.

IBM's Institute for Business Value found that by late 2024 only 8% of banks were developing generative AI in a systematic, enterprise-wide way, while 78% remained tactical. McKinsey's November 2025 State of AI survey found only 6% of organisations qualified as high performers, attributing more than 5% of EBIT to AI. PwC's 2026 AI Performance Study found 74% of AI's economic value being captured by 20% of organisations.

That is not democratisation. That is concentration.

## The use-case portfolio has had its day

The first phase of AI adoption had a pleasing logic to it. Collect use cases, score them, fund the best, let enthusiasm find the value. It was attractive because it felt empirical and low risk. Nobody had to bet the operating model. Nobody had to tell a senior business line that its process design, data ownership and control environment were now part of the same conversation.

The problem is that a portfolio of isolated use cases is a poor unit of change. McKinsey's December 2024 work on rewiring the enterprise makes the sharper point: value sits in business domains, not in scattered pilots. A typical bank has roughly 25 subdomains, with fewer than ten driving 70 to 80% of incremental value. If that is true, then the sensible question is not "how many AI ideas do we have?" It is "which domains are worth rewiring end to end?"

KPMG's Q1 2026 pulse says scaling, not funding, is now the primary barrier, cited by 65% of leaders. That should make boards twitchy. Money is the easy part of hard change. Scaling means integration, controls, ownership, incentives, workflow redesign, and the quiet death of pet experiments.

The industry has a phrase for the alternative: thousand flowers. It sounds generous until you see the garden maintenance bill.

McKinsey has warned about "POC hell". Metadata Weekly reported that 42% of companies abandoned most AI initiatives in 2025, up from 17% in 2024, with 95% of pilots never reaching production. Bain found that only 15 to 25% of finance functions have scaled AI to full production. Different source bases, same smell. Pilot proliferation consumes IT, data, security and compliance attention before it produces aggregate value.

A proof of concept is useful when it de-risks a decision. It is theatre when it avoids one.

## Scale belongs to the firms doing the dull work

JPMorgan is the clearest public case of scale tied to foundations. Its $17 billion technology budget, 2,000 AI and ML specialists, and internal factory architecture produced disclosed test-and-control outcomes: $220 million from AI-driven credit card upgrades and $100 million from commercial bank suggestion tools. Derek Waldron's account of LLM Suite reaching 100,000 employees within months of its 2024 launch, with just under half using GenAI daily, gives the adoption story teeth.

HSBC tells a different, and more European, version of the same story. Under Georges Elhedery, the bank decommissioned 1,165 legacy applications in 2025, 36% of a four-year target, alongside AI deployment. It also reported a $1.8 billion cost reallocation and 1,800 net new technology hires. This is simultaneous surgery, remove complexity while changing the operating rhythm.

It may still fail. FSI Digital Weekly characterised HSBC as a cautionary case, spending around 10% of revenue on technology while devolving into silos. That counterpoint matters. Large spend does not absolve poor organisation. It can hide it for longer.

But at least the right problem is in view. If the estate remains fragmented, the data remains unclear, and control ownership remains scattered, AI becomes another layer of expensive ambiguity. The model is not the system. The system is the system.

## Governance has left the CIO's waiting room

The governance story has also changed. AI in financial services is no longer a clever technology problem looking for permission. It is a business control problem with technology inside it.

MIT CISR's December 2025 finding is uncomfortable: only 26% of large-company boards meet updated AI-savvy criteria, but those companies average 10.9 percentage points above industry-average return on equity. PwC found AI leaders are 1.5 times more likely to have a cross-functional AI governance board. McKinsey's March 2025 data found AI governance jointly owned by two leaders on average, with board involvement at only 17% of large firms.

That gap is where programmes go to become slide-deck archaeology.

Regulators are not offering an escape route. The UK FCA confirmed in December 2025 that it will not introduce AI-specific rules, relying on Consumer Duty and SM&CR. The US GAO's May 2025 report confirmed that SR 11-7 model risk management principles apply to AI. Bhattacharyya et al.'s 2025 arXiv paper mapped generative-AI validation to SR 11-7.

The message is plain enough. You do not get a new governance universe because the model can write fluent prose. Existing accountability still applies. Model risk becomes a programme gate, not a clean-up task after the demo has charmed the room.

## The productivity story has a hole in it

The strongest objection is that the technology might simply get good enough to overwhelm the organisational drag. If agents can do the work, perhaps the operating model can catch up later. This is the intoxicating version of the story, and it is not baseless.

McKinsey's February 2026 work on agentic AI in banking operations estimates credit-analyst productivity gains of 20 to 60% from multiagent systems, with 30% faster decision-making, and projects up to four percentage points of ROTE uplift for AI pioneers. Marianne Lake of JPMorgan has publicly projected 40 to 50% productivity gains in operations. Bain's primary survey of 109 US financial services firms found an average 20% productivity gain and average AI investment of $22.1 million per firm in 2024.

Google's DORA programme, renamed the State of AI-Assisted Software Development in 2025, found that AI acts as an amplifier of existing engineering conditions, with high-quality internal platforms the decisive differentiator. METR's July 2025 randomised controlled trial of experienced developers found a 19% slowdown when AI tools were permitted, while those same developers estimated themselves 20% faster. METR's capability-arc work found task-completion horizons doubling every seven months since 2019, reaching 50-minute autonomous tasks at 50% reliability in early 2025.

That is the contradiction in miniature. AI can be improving quickly and still be negative in poorly framed work. People can feel faster while being slower. Narrow, instrumented workflows can produce gains while general-purpose deployment adds drag.

The likely answer is not that the optimists are fools or the sceptics are right by default. It is that productivity is not a property of the tool alone. It is a property of the tool inside a system.

Financial services should understand this better than most sectors. A faster analyst in a broken credit process may only produce faster queueing. A better coding assistant in a weak engineering environment may increase review burden. A clever operations agent without clean exception handling may create a new class of invisible work. The gain appears where the workflow, data, controls and feedback loops are ready to receive it.

Everywhere else, it generates plausible activity.

## The next constraint is managerial

The people problem is also being misdiagnosed. Specialist hiring matters, but the bottleneck is moving into middle management, where priorities, workflow design and behavioural permission live.

Lloyds Group COO Ron van Kemenade sent the top 300 leaders to Cambridge. Bank of America runs firm-wide AI learning pathways. The Financial Services Skills Commission's May 2025 report found one-size-fits-all upskilling to be the dominant failure mode. That is the clue. Sending everyone the same content is administratively neat. It does not change how a claims manager, risk owner, product lead and engineering manager make decisions on Monday morning.

Stack Overflow's 2025 survey of 49,000 developers found AI usage at 84% but trust at 29%, down from 40% the previous year, with only 17% reporting improved team collaboration. Sentiment is shifting. Behaviour is not.

The workforce data points in the same direction. Fortune's December 2025 analysis found Bank of America employed only four fewer workers than a year earlier, JPMorgan added 2,000 employees, over a third in corporate operations, and Goldman Sachs' headcount was 1,800 higher year-on-year despite multiple layoff rounds. Randstad reported a 24% decline in junior financial services roles since January 2024, with data-entry clerks down 63%.

So the near-term labour story is not mass disappearance. It is hiring suppression, role reshaping and a harsher entry ramp. Work does not vanish neatly. It moves, often into the judgement layer. That makes the manager more consequential, not less.

This is where many AI programmes will underperform. They will buy tools for specialists, announce productivity targets, and treat management capability as change communications. Then they will wonder why pilots stall at the boundary between a capable team and an unreformed process.

## Fewer pilots, fewer alibis

The hard lesson from 2024 to 2026 is that AI value in financial services is becoming less mysterious and less evenly distributed. Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance found only 40% of respondents reporting increased profitability from AI, while 43% reported no change. It also found 62% of organisations spending over $100,000 annually reaching advanced maturity, against 39% of lower spenders. Forrester reported that only 20% of businesses saw earnings benefits from 2023 AI investments. Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise found only one in five companies has mature governance for autonomous agents.

The pattern is not kind. Firms with capital, architecture, governance, domain focus and management attention are pulling away. Firms treating AI as a bundle of tools are collecting anecdotes.

There is still room for caution. Gary Marcus has argued that banking is exposed to the AI investment cycle and judged agents to have mostly disappointed by August 2025. He may be early, wrong, or annoyingly right in selected places. The open tension is real: PwC's value concentration may be a temporary adoption curve, or it may be the shape of the market after the easy experiments have burned off.

I suspect the second possibility deserves more respect than it gets.

Not because only the richest institutions can use AI, although JPMorgan-scale investment is not a rounding error. Not because agents are useless, which the better evidence does not support. The reason is simpler. AI rewards the parts of the organisation that were already hard to fake: clear ownership, clean platforms, credible controls, capable managers and the discipline to stop doing things.

The most telling AI metric in banking may not be model accuracy, prompt volume or licences issued. It may be the number of old applications retired, the number of pilots killed, and the number of senior leaders who can explain which domain they are changing and why.

The thousand flowers have wilted. Good. Now we can see the weeds.
