# The Productivity Gains Are in the Post

*The numbers banks are quoting are real in narrow, well-instrumented workflows and imaginary almost everywhere else. Those are not the same programme.*

In July 2025, METR ran a randomised controlled trial on experienced open-source developers working in their own codebases. With AI tools permitted, they were nineteen per cent slower. Asked afterwards how they had done, the same developers estimated they had been twenty per cent faster. That is a forty-point swing between what happened and what it felt like, recorded under controlled conditions, by people who write software for a living.

Hold that figure next to the ones the industry is selling itself. Marianne Lake of JPMorgan has publicly projected forty to fifty per cent productivity gains in operations. McKinsey's February 2026 work on agentic banking estimates credit-analyst gains of twenty to sixty per cent. These are not lies. They are also not measuring the same thing METR measured, and the distance between the two is the most useful piece of information in the entire financial services AI corpus. Most programme business cases are quietly built on the felt number.

## The category error is treating productivity as a property of the tool

The instinct is to argue about which figure is true, as though one camp has good data and the other has marketing. That is the wrong fight. Google's DORA programme, renamed the State of AI-Assisted Software Development in 2025 and running on nearly five thousand respondents, landed on the finding that resolves it: AI acts as an amplifier of existing engineering conditions, and the decisive differentiator is the quality of the internal platform underneath it. AI does not deliver a productivity number. It multiplies whatever number your operating model already produces, including the negative ones.

This reframes the METR result. Nineteen per cent slower is not a verdict on the models. It is a verdict on dropping capable tools into a general-purpose environment with no instrumentation, no test-and-control, and no platform tuned to feed them. Forty to sixty per cent is what you get in a narrow workflow that has been engineered, measured, and fenced. The reconciliation the evidence base itself reaches is the honest one: gains are real where the workflow is well-instrumented and negative in general deployment. Same technology, opposite outcomes, and the variable is the organisation, not the model.

## The only credible outcome metrics are infrastructure-first

If that frame is right, the disclosed numbers should cluster around the firms that built the platform before they counted the gains. They do. JPMorgan's seventeen billion dollar technology budget and two thousand AI and ML specialists sit behind the only test-and-control outcomes anyone has published: two hundred and twenty million dollars from AI-driven credit card upgrades, one hundred million from commercial bank suggestion tools. LLM Suite reached a hundred thousand employees within months of its 2024 launch, with just under half using it daily. That is not a pilot that escaped. It is a factory with a meter on the output.

HSBC, under Georges Elhedery, decommissioned one thousand one hundred and sixty-five legacy applications in 2025 alone, thirty-six per cent of a four-year target, while deploying AI rather than after. It is the clearest public case of a bank refusing to phase foundation work behind use-case delivery. The contrast with its own past is instructive: FSI Digital Weekly characterises the older HSBC as a cautionary tale of spending around ten per cent of revenue on technology while devolving into silos. Same firm, same spend bracket, different operating discipline, different result. The money was never the binding constraint.

That is Lean, stated plainly. Jevons tells you AI lowers the marginal cost of a feature and raises feasible supply. It does not tell you the constraint disappears. It moves. The expensive thing is no longer the code. It is the instrumented workflow that lets you prove the code did something, and the platform that lets the next workflow reuse the proof. Brooks said it in 1986 and the evidence keeps re-deriving it: there is no silver bullet, only the accidental complexity you have already paid down.

## The thousand flowers pattern is a recognised failure mode, not a phase

The opposite of infrastructure-first is the pilot garden, and its numbers are brutal. A Metadata Weekly figure puts the share of companies abandoning most AI initiatives at forty-two per cent in 2025, up from seventeen the year before, with ninety-five per cent of pilots never reaching production. Bain finds only fifteen to twenty-five per cent of finance functions have scaled AI to full production. KPMG's Q1 2026 pulse reports that scaling, not funding, is now the primary barrier, cited by sixty-five per cent of leaders.

The distribution this produces is bimodal and ugly. PwC's 2026 study finds seventy-four per cent of AI's economic value captured by twenty per cent of organisations. McKinsey's November 2025 survey finds only six per cent of organisations qualify as high performers attributing more than five per cent of EBIT to AI. IBM's Institute for Business Value found only eight per cent of banks were building generative AI in a systematic, enterprise-wide way by late 2024, with seventy-eight per cent still tactical. The Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance, in the largest cross-stakeholder survey of the period, found only forty per cent reporting increased profitability and forty-three per cent reporting no change at all. Whether that split is an early-adoption curve flattening out or a structural endpoint is genuinely unresolved. I do not know yet, and neither, on the published evidence, does anyone else.

## The bottleneck is middle management, and the trust line is going the wrong way

The people data is where the felt-versus-measured gap turns into a programme risk. Stack Overflow's 2025 survey of forty-nine thousand developers found AI usage at eighty-four per cent but trust at twenty-nine per cent, down from forty the previous year, with only seventeen per cent reporting improved team collaboration. Adoption is rising while confidence falls. The Financial Services Skills Commission's May 2025 report names one-size-fits-all upskilling as the dominant failure mode, which is precisely the mode most banks default to. Lloyds sent its top three hundred leaders to Cambridge; that is the exception being reported, not the rule.

Meanwhile the workforce is being reshaped by hiring suppression rather than redundancy. Randstad reports a twenty-four per cent decline in junior financial services roles since January 2024, data-entry clerks down sixty-three per cent. Bank of America employed four fewer people than a year earlier. The juniors who would have built tomorrow's mental models are not being made redundant. They are simply not being hired, which is a slower and less visible way to hollow out the same capability.

## Ask to see the test and the control

There is a named position here, and it is not scepticism about AI. The technology works. METR's own capability-arc work has task-completion horizons doubling every seven months since 2019. The position is narrower and harder to argue with: a productivity figure you cannot trace to a test-and-control workflow is a felt number, and felt numbers belong in the marketing budget, not the business case. JPMorgan can show you the control group. Most programmes quoting fifty per cent cannot, and the ones that cannot are usually quoting METR's developers without knowing it.

So when the next deck lands with forty to sixty per cent on the title slide, the only question worth asking is the one Deming would have asked. Where is the control group, and what did it do. If the answer is a confident smile, you are not looking at a transformation programme. You are looking at the post arriving, eventually, possibly, addressed to someone else.
