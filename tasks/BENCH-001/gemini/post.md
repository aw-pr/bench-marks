# The 8% Economy

Why most AI programmes are delivering nothing, and what the handful of winners are doing differently.

---

In the early 20th century, the automotive industry wasn’t dominated by giants. It was a chaotic landscape of hundreds of small workshops, each producing a few cars a year. They competed on craft, on bespoke features, on the personality of their founder. Then Ford opened the River Rouge plant, and the game changed. It wasn’t just a faster workshop. It was a new system of production, built on principles of flow and standardisation that the craft-based shops couldn’t replicate. Within a few years, most of them were gone.

We are seeing the same pattern play out today with artificial intelligence, but we are mistaking it for something else. We think we are in a gold rush, where anyone with a pan and some gumption can find a nugget. The evidence suggests this is wrong. This isn't a gold rush; it's the building of the railroads. And a tiny fraction of organisations are capturing almost all of the economic value, not by finding shiny use-cases, but by doing the boring, foundational work of building the industrial base for this new era.

The numbers are stark. A late 2024 study from IBM’s Institute for Business Value found only 8% of banks were developing generative AI in a systematic, enterprise-wide way. The other 78% were still stuck in tactical, one-off projects. This 8% figure is the key. It points to a deep bifurcation in competence. It’s a winner-takes-most market, and the winners declared their strategy years ago. PwC’s 2026 AI Performance Study confirms the outcome: 74% of AI's economic value is being captured by just 20% of organisations. For the rest, the returns are flat. The Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance found that while 40% of firms reported increased profitability from AI, a stubbornly high 43% reported no change at all.

This isn’t a temporary feature of an early market. It looks structural. And the reason is that most organisations have been pursuing a strategy that is now a recognised failure mode.

### The Thousand Flowers Fallacy

For the last few years, the default approach to AI has been to "let a thousand flowers bloom". The idea was to sprinkle seeds across the organisation, run dozens of pilots and proofs-of-concept, and wait to see what grew. The business case was seductive: low initial investment, lots of visible activity, and the promise of a breakout "killer app".

It has been a catastrophic failure. McKinsey has been warning about "POC hell" for years, and the data now proves them right. One Substack, Metadata Weekly, reported a figure that 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives in 2025, a sharp increase from 17% the previous year. An astonishing 95% of pilots never reach production. Bain’s work reinforces the diagnosis, finding only 15–25% of finance functions have scaled AI to full production.

This isn't just a waste of money. It’s a waste of the one resource you cannot buy more of: organisational focus. Each of those doomed pilots consumed IT and compliance resources. Each one needed data, governance, and management attention. The collective effort falls below the aggregate value threshold, leaving the organisation exhausted and cynical. The thousand-flowers approach doesn't produce a beautiful garden; it produces a field of weeds and a depleted budget.

The alternative isn't more glamour, but less. It’s a strategic decision to treat AI not as a portfolio of lottery tickets, but as a fundamental rewiring of the enterprise. This means abandoning the use-case portfolio as a design pattern. As McKinsey argued back in December 2024, material value requires transforming entire business domains from end to end. The handful of organisations driving real returns are the ones that made this choice.

### Boring Work, Billion-Dollar Returns

Look at the high performers. They aren't running more pilots. They are building factories.

JPMorgan Chase is the canonical example. With a technology budget of $17 billion and a team of 2,000 AI and machine learning specialists, they built an internal factory architecture. The results are not vague promises of future productivity; they are disclosed, test-and-control outcomes. They can point to $220 million generated from AI-driven credit card upgrades and another $100 million from suggestion tools in their commercial bank. When their internal LLM Suite launched in 2024, it reached 100,000 employees within months, with just under half using generative AI daily. That isn’t a pilot. That is industrial scale.

Or look at HSBC. In 2025, under Georges Elhedery, the bank decommissioned 1,165 legacy applications. This was not a side project; it was 36% of a four-year target, running in parallel with their AI deployment. It’s the most explicit public example of a bank choosing to do the hard foundation work and deliver value simultaneously, rather than sequencing them. KPMG’s Q1 2026 pulse survey backs this up: for 65% of leaders, the primary barrier to AI value is no longer funding, but scaling.

This is the real divide. The 8% are not smarter, and their algorithms are not magical. They are simply doing the work everyone else wants to skip. They are consolidating platforms, cleaning data, and decommissioning legacy systems. They are treating model risk management as a programme gate, not a deployment afterthought, aligning with existing frameworks like the US GAO's application of SR 11-7, as regulators have signalled. They are moving governance out of the CIO’s office and putting it under the CEO or COO, where it belongs.

They are also tackling the real bottleneck: people. Not the lack of PhDs in machine learning, but the capability of middle management and the trust of the workforce. While Bank of America runs firm-wide AI learning pathways and Lloyds sends its top 300 leaders to Cambridge, the data shows a deep problem. According to Stack Overflow’s 2025 survey of nearly fifty thousand developers, AI usage is at 84%, but trust in the tools has collapsed to 29%, down from 40% the year before. Only 17% reported improved team collaboration. Sentiment might be shifting in the executive suite, but behaviour on the ground is not.

This trust gap helps explain the so-called productivity paradox. How can JPMorgan project 40–50% productivity gains in operations while a randomised controlled trial from METR finds experienced developers are 19% *slower* when using AI tools? The answer comes from Google's DORA programme. Its 2025 report concluded that AI acts as an amplifier of existing engineering conditions. With high-quality internal platforms and clean data, it boosts output. Dropped into a complex, low-trust environment, it creates friction, confusion, and negative productivity. The gains are real, but only for those who have earned them.

### There Is No Silver Bullet

The next wave of hype around agentic AI will only widen this gap. McKinsey projects productivity gains of 20–60% for credit analysts using multi-agent systems, with the potential for a four-percentage-point uplift in return on tangible equity for pioneers. Yet Deloitte’s 2026 research shows only one company in five has mature governance for the autonomous agents they are already deploying. We are building the next floor of the skyscraper before the foundations of the first are dry.

For most organisations, the AI programme is already a failure. They are playing a different game from the winners, focused on pilots while the leaders build infrastructure. They are mesmerised by vendor productivity claims while their own developers’ trust in the tools evaporates. They are hoping for a killer app to emerge from the chaos, not realising that the winners decided years ago that the system *is* the app.

The path to joining the 8% is not a mystery. It's just unpopular. It involves saying no to a hundred plausible ideas to focus on the one enabling platform. It means allocating more budget to decommissioning old systems than to launching new chatbots. It means accepting, as HSBC has, that your technology spending might be a tenth of JPMorgan's, so your only hope of competing is through brutal focus.

The AI revolution will not be won by the team with the most PowerPoint decks or the longest list of pilots. It will be won in the engine room, by the organisations with the discipline to do the boring work first. For everyone else, it’s just faster horses.
